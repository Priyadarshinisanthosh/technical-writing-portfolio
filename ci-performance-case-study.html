From 42 Minutes to 8: How a Platform Team Rebuilt Its CI Critical Path

Portfolio simulation: The company, quotes, repository, and measurements are fictional. The workflow patterns and editorial process demonstrate how I would develop an evidence-led customer story with engineers.

The Editorial Brief

Audience: platform engineers and engineering leaders responsible for slow, expensive CI pipelines.

Reader promise: a reproducible way to identify the CI critical path—not a list of generic “speed up CI” tips.

Editorial position: adding more runners does not fix work that should not be on the critical path. First remove unnecessary work, then parallelize and cache what remains.

Engineering Interview Plan

  • What did developers experience before the change, and how was it measured?
  • Which job determined merge latency at the p50 and p95?
  • What did the team try that failed or produced negligible improvement?
  • How were cache keys selected, invalidated, and monitored?
  • Which jobs were safe to run concurrently, and which dependencies were real?
  • What trade-offs appeared in compute cost, flakiness, or debugging?
  • Can logs, workflow diffs, and dashboards substantiate every performance claim?

Sample Published Story

The bottleneck was the dependency graph

The team’s pull-request workflow looked parallel in the GitHub Actions UI, but one serial chain—dependency installation, build, integration tests, then packaging—controlled when a change could merge. Faster runners improved individual jobs without shortening the full chain enough to matter.

The platform team mapped each job by duration, dependency, failure rate, and cache behavior. That made two hidden problems visible: packaging ran on pull requests even though no artifact was consumed, and integration-test groups waited for one another despite using isolated test data.

Make the workflow express the real dependencies

jobs:
  build:
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4
      - uses: actions/cache@v4
        with:
          path: ~/.cache/build
          key: build-${{ runner.os }}-${{ hashFiles('**/lockfile') }}

  integration:
    needs: build
    strategy:
      fail-fast: false
      matrix:
        suite: [accounts, billing, search]

The revised workflow removed packaging from the pull-request path, restored dependencies from a content-addressed cache, and split integration tests into independent matrix jobs. Concurrency rules cancelled superseded runs on the same branch, reducing queue pressure without hiding failures on the latest commit.

What did not work

A broad cache key initially produced high hit rates but occasionally restored incompatible dependencies. The team narrowed the key to the runner OS and lockfile hash, then added an explicit restore-key policy. The more accurate cache had a slightly lower hit rate and far fewer invalid restores.

Takeaway

Optimize developer waiting time, not isolated job duration. Measure the path that gates merge, verify which dependencies are real, and publish enough methodology for another team to challenge—or reproduce—the result.
